Chapter 1

A Swedish Boy in Greeley

from Saving Otto Brune · by Ross A. Lindberg
◷ 5 min read
“Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new.”— 2 Corinthians 5:17 (KJV)

My father was born on June 19, 1912, in Greeley, Colorado, the last child in a family that had crossed an ocean.

His father, Adolph Lindberg, had been born on February 1, 1861, in the Västra Götaland region of Sweden. His mother, Ida, born Ida Erickson on September 2, 1867, came from Dalsland, also in Sweden. Both came to America in the late 1800s, part of that great wave of Scandinavian families who left the old country in search of work and land in the American West. Like so many of them, they ended up in the farm-and-railroad towns of northern Colorado, and they stayed.

Adolph worked for the Union Pacific Railroad for the whole of his working life. That was steady, honest labor, and it fed his family. But according to my father, Adolph was not a man who knew how to talk to his children. He was a loner, quiet and distant. He provided, but he did not engage. My father never said it with bitterness, but I could hear the ache in it when he talked about it: a boy who wanted a father to teach him things, and got a man who went to work and came home and kept to himself.

There were six children in the Lindberg household. First came Fred, then Paul, then Marie and Esther. After Esther came a little boy named Erik, who died as an infant before my father was born. Then, last of all, came Edward, the baby of the family.

When a boy cannot find what he needs in his father, he will find it somewhere else. My father found it in his oldest brother, Fred. Fred was the one who showed him the things a boy has to learn: how to throw, how to catch, how to take a hard grounder and keep your eye on the ball. They shared a love for baseball, and in those years there was only one name that mattered in baseball: Babe Ruth. My father idolized him, as did half the boys in America. But my father was not content to just admire. He became a very good ballplayer himself, good enough to stand out in school and in every athletic group he joined.

I would see the athlete in him decades later. He was the first one picked for any team, and he would be the first one picked for the baseball games the medics organized behind the lines in Europe. But that comes later. For now, he was a boy from a Swedish household in Greeley, throwing a ball with his big brother, and learning that someone cared.

The Rebel

By his teenage years my father had a reputation, and he was the first to admit he earned it. He told me he was “a bit of a rabble-rouser.” He liked the dances. He smoked cigarettes. He drank. He ran with a crowd that lived fast, and he lived somewhat wildly, by his own account.

If you had asked anyone in Greeley in those days to predict how Edward Lindberg’s life would turn out, I doubt many would have said: Bible college, ministry, hero of the medical corps. But there was one person who never stopped believing something different about him. She was praying.

My grandmother Ida was a devout Christian all of her life. She went to church on Sundays and on Wednesdays, as faithfully as the sun came up. She read her Bible every day. And every day, according to what my father told me, she prayed for her youngest son. She prayed that he would put down the cigarettes, leave the dance halls, and turn his heart toward God.

The church was the Church of God, a movement whose roots ran back to Anderson, Indiana, where it had established its headquarters and its Bible college. The family attended faithfully for as long as my father could remember. His sisters and his mother were there week after week, and in time, he came with them. In later years that Greeley congregation became Broadview Park Church of God, on 35th Avenue. The building has since closed, and today it belongs to the American Legion.

Nineteen

In 1931, when he was nineteen years old, my father went to church with his mother, and something happened that he never forgot.

There was an altar call that day. The pastor invited anyone who felt the Holy Spirit drawing them to come forward and give their life to Jesus Christ. My father felt it. He told me it was a conviction, not a loud one, but impossible to ignore. And this young man who had spent his teens running from the church got up from his seat, walked to the front, and knelt. The pastor prayed with him as he asked Jesus Christ to be his Savior.

My grandmother’s prayers had been answered.

That was the moment everything changed. My father often described it using the words of the Apostle Paul: he became a new creature in Christ. He quit the bars. He quit the dances and the late nights. He quit the cigarettes. From that day forward, he gave himself completely, in his own words one hundred percent, to growing in the Lord. He read his Bible every day. He prayed every day. He and his mother attended church together, along with his sisters.

I want you to notice something about that story. It was not an emotional episode that burned hot and faded. My father kept that commitment for the next seventy-six years. He read his Bible every day for the rest of his life. When he died in 2007, it was the same King James Bible he’d held when he was young, worn soft by decades of use.

He did not know that in a few years he would be in a Bible college, or that in thirteen years he would be in Normandy. He only knew that he had been found.

And as I will tell you in the next chapter, he was about to meet two women who would shape the rest of his life: one who broke his heart, and one who mended it.
