Prologue

The Pin

from Saving Otto Brune · by Ross A. Lindberg
◷ 3 min read
“But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you.”— Matthew 5:44 (KJV)

There is a small object in my family’s keeping that weighs almost nothing and carries more than I can put into words. It is a pin, a Hitler Youth badge, the kind awarded for service in the organization the Nazi Party built for the young people of Germany between 1933 and 1945. A thing like that belongs in a museum case with a card beside it explaining what it was. In our family it is something else. It is a reminder.

My father took it from a dying German soldier on the night of July 16, 1945, in a railway wreck near Aßling, Germany.

My father, Sergeant Edward G. Lindberg of the United States Army Medical Department, did not take it as a trophy. He took it so that he would never forget the man he had held while he died, and so that someone, someday, would know the man’s story. The soldier’s name, I believe, was Otto Brune.

I was the youngest of three children. My brother and sister were seventeen and thirteen years older than I was, and by the time I was old enough to listen, the war was already a long way behind my father. But he told me about it, more than he ever told them. I think, looking back, that I became a kind of therapist to him without either of us knowing it. We fished together. We golfed together. And somewhere in between, on the porch or in the car or walking down a fairway, he would let a piece of it out, a little at a time, and I would listen.

Out of everything he told me, one night stood above the rest. My father had seen men torn apart by mortar fire and artillery. He had amputated limbs, sutured wounds, dug shrapnel from flesh and pushed morphine into men who were screaming. He told me that of all the horrors he had witnessed in the war, none was worse than what he saw that night in Aßling. “A scene from hell,” he called it, and he was not a man given to saying things like that.

This book is the story of how a boy from Greeley, Colorado, the son of Swedish immigrants, a man who refused to carry a weapon into the greatest war in history, came to be kneeling in the wreckage of a train, in the rain, in the dark, holding the hand of an enemy soldier who was cursing him. It is the story of what my father said to that man, and of the last word the man spoke.

It is also the story of what that night did to my father, and of the family he came home to. And it is the story of what he passed to me.

I have told parts of this to a few people over the years. Now I want to tell all of it, because I believe my father’s story is a testimony that is bigger than he was. If you finish this book with one thought, I hope it is this:

there is no one on this earth, not even the man who curses you while you try to save him, who is beyond the reach of the love of God.

Let me begin where my father began.
